Mental Imagery & The Aphantasia Spectrum

Why some people see vivid internal worlds, others catch only brief flashes — and why that may share a neural root with how ADHD rewires attention.

The Spectrum

Mental imagery vividness varies enormously between people.

It is not binary. It runs from complete absence (aphantasia) to imagery that rivals real perception (hyperphantasia). The vast majority of people land somewhere in between, yet many never realise how different their inner visual world is from those around them.

Static is not absence — it is unguided activity

The static in aphantasia is not silence. It represents the visual cortex's spontaneous resting-state firing — constant background activity without any top-down signal shaping it into a recognisable representation. The dark, near-void quality of low imagery between bursts captures the same idea: the cortex is active, but the PFC-driven feedback signal is too weak or too brief to steer it into a stable image.

The smoothness of the noise (rather than pure per-pixel randomness) reflects the spatial correlation of cortical activity — neurons fire in local clusters, producing smooth-edged patches rather than salt-and-pepper noise.

It is not just visual — the multisensory picture

Aphantasia is rarely confined to vision. Most people who report no mind's eye also report a quieter inner ear, fewer imagined smells and tastes, and weaker imagined movement. The same top-down machinery appears to underwrite imagery across every sense, so when it is dialled down it tends to be dialled down everywhere.

  • Anauralia — absence of an inner voice or imagined sound. Most self-identified visual aphantasics also score low on auditory imagery measures.
  • Olfactory, gustatory, tactile — reduced ability to summon a smell, a taste, or the feel of a texture from memory.
  • Motor imagery — weaker rehearsal of movement “from the inside,” relevant to skill learning and sport.
  • Dreaming — many aphantasics still dream visually but report less control and less sensory richness while awake.

Hinwar & Lambert, 2021 (anauralia) · Dawes, Keogh & Pearson, 2020 / 2024 (multisensory subtypes)

Bottom line

Mental imagery exists on a spectrum driven by the same underlying machinery — the brain's ability to sustain a top-down feedback signal to visual cortex without real visual input. Vividness reflects signal strength and persistence, not intelligence or imagination.

How It Was Discovered

Francis Galton first documented radical individual variation in imagery vividness in 1880, shocked that many eminent colleagues reported no imagery whatsoever. The phenomenon was largely forgotten until Adam Zeman (University of Exeter) re-described and named aphantasia in 2015 after a patient lost his imagery following cardiac surgery. The field has grown quickly since, with large-scale internet surveys revealing the spectrum is far wider than clinical samples suggested.

Mental Imagery Is Top-Down Vision

The brain runs its visual system in reverse

Normal vision is bottom-up: photons hit the retina → signals travel through the lateral geniculate nucleus → V1/V2 visual cortex → higher processing areas (object recognition, spatial processing, meaning). Each stage adds interpretation.

Mental imagery inverts this. Higher-order areas — the prefrontal cortex (PFC) and parietal cortex — send signals back down into early visual cortex (V1/V2), reconstructing an image representation without any incoming light. This is called top-down modulation or feedback activation.

The strength and persistence of that downward feedback signal is what separates vivid imagery from fragmented snapshots from no imagery at all.

The visuospatial sketchpad

Working memory contains a dedicated subsystem — the visuospatial sketchpad (Baddeley, 1974) — that holds and manipulates visual and spatial information. For imagery to persist, this system must keep refreshing the visual cortex representation faster than it naturally decays.

Think of it like a display that needs a redraw signal every ~200ms to keep showing the image. In high-imagery people, that redraw arrives reliably. In low-imagery people, it arrives too weakly or too late. In aphantasia, it may not arrive in the visual cortex at all.

Beyond V1 — Where the Image Is Built

The Fusiform Imagery Node

For decades the standard model placed mental imagery squarely in early visual cortex (V1/V2) — the same maps used to see real images, reactivated from above. Newer evidence complicates that picture. A meta-analysis of 27 neuroimaging studies converges on a region in the left ventral temporal cortex, in and around the fusiform gyrus, that activates specifically during imagery and that a Bayesian re-analysis suggests is sufficient even when early visual cortex is not engaged. The authors named it the Fusiform Imagery Node (FIN).

Lesion evidence supports the same conclusion: vivid imagery can survive damage to the occipital lobe, but tends to fail when left temporal cortex is damaged. A 2026 lesion-network mapping study of acquired aphantasia found the responsible lesions, scattered across many brain locations, all connect into a network that converges on the FIN.

The current best model is therefore three-stage, not two: prefrontal cortex issues the intent, the FIN assembles the categorical and featural content of the image, and that content is then projected back into early visual cortex to give it perceptual texture and detail. Damage anywhere along this chain can flatten imagery; the visualizer's static represents what V1 looks like when the upstream chain never reaches it.

Spagna, Hajhajate, Liu & Bartolomeo, 2021 (Neurosci Biobehav Rev) · Bartolomeo et al., 2020 (Nat Rev Neurosci) · Kutsche et al., 2026 (Cortex)

The cortical-excitability paradox

The intuitive guess — bigger, louder visual cortex equals stronger imagery — turns out to be backwards. Across multiple studies, the people with the most vivid mental images tend to have a smaller and less excitable primary visual cortex paired with a larger frontal cortex and stronger functional connectivity between the two.

  • Smaller V1 surface area predicts stronger (though slightly less precise) imagery.
  • Lower V1 cortical excitability predicts stronger imagery; artificially lowering V1 excitability with brain stimulation increases imagery vividness.
  • V1 and prefrontal cortex sit in a reciprocal anatomical trade-off — as one gets bigger across individuals, the other tends to shrink.
  • Hyperphantasics show measurably stronger functional connectivity between prefrontal areas (Brodmann 9, 10, 11) and visual cortex than aphantasics.

A plausible read: a quieter, less twitchy V1 is a cleaner canvas. When the canvas is already noisy, the top-down signal has to fight resting activity to be seen. When it is quiet, even a modest descending signal can paint a vivid image. This is precisely the dynamic the visualizer dramatises — the “static” modes are V1 firing without effective descending control to ride over it.

Bergmann et al., 2016 (Cereb Cortex) · Song, Schwarzkopf, Kanai & Rees, 2011 (J Neurosci) · Keogh, Bergmann & Pearson, 2020 (eLife) · Milton et al., 2021 (Cereb Cortex Comm)

The ~200ms Timescale

The “snapshot” quality of low imagery — a brief flash of image that dissolves — maps precisely onto the known timescales of visual memory and working memory refresh.

ProcessTimescaleRelevance
Iconic memory decay~150–300msHow long a visual trace persists without active maintenance
Working memory refresh cycle~200–250msHow often the visuospatial sketchpad redraws its held content
V1 → PFC → V1 feedback loop~80–150msRound-trip delay for the top-down re-activation signal
Gamma oscillation cycle (visual binding)~25–40ms (40Hz)The neural carrier wave that synchronises the image representation

For imagery to persist, the refresh signal must arrive before iconic memory decays. High-imagery people win this race consistently. Low-imagery people lose it — resulting in one working-memory cycle of image, then a gap, then another attempt that also collapses.

Why Images Drift and Dissolve

The directive system vs. the generative system

Deliberate imagery requires the prefrontal executive system to continuously maintain the intended template — holding “this is what I want to see” against all competing signals. When that hold weakens even briefly, a second system takes over.

The default mode network (DMN) — active during mind-wandering, daydreaming, and memory retrieval — runs on associations and emotional salience rather than intent. It does not know what you meant to imagine. It generates the next most associated or most emotionally salient representation.

This is why an apple drifts to apple-adjacent, then something unrelated, then dissolves. The image did not fail — control transferred from the directive to the generative system, and the generative system follows its own logic, not yours.

High vs. low imagery: the same system, different control

High-imagery people use the DMN too — it is part of imagery generation. But their PFC hold on the intended template is strong enough to steer the DMN rather than be overridden by it. They can give the DMN a brief and it follows it.

Low-imagery people lose PFC hold faster, so the DMN drifts freely. The imagery does not disappear because something is missing — it drifts because the steering signal is not persistent enough to keep the generative system on topic.

Hypothesis This section presents an emerging theoretical linkage between low mental imagery and ADHD. The mechanistic argument is grounded in established neuroscience, but the direct empirical connection has not yet been confirmed by controlled studies. It is a framework for thinking — not a settled diagnosis or clinical claim.
The Shared Failure Point

Both conditions converge on prefrontal signal persistence

Low imagery and ADHD inattention appear unrelated at first glance — one seems perceptual, the other behavioural. But both point at the same anatomical bottleneck: the prefrontal cortex's ability to hold a signal stable over time.

Domain What PFC must do What failure looks like
Mental imagery Sustain top-down feedback to V1/V2 across multiple refresh cycles ~200ms flash, then static — image cannot chain frame to frame
ADHD inattention Hold a task-relevant representation stable against intrusive competing signals Attention derails, task-goal fades, DMN intrudes mid-task
Working memory Refresh a held representation faster than it naturally decays Information evaporates mid-task; losing the thread of thought

These are not three separate failures. They may be the same failure — PFC signal persistence — showing up in three different output domains simultaneously.

Dopamine: The Throughline

Why PFC signal fades in both conditions

In the prefrontal cortex, dopamine acting on D1 receptors performs a specific function: it biases pyramidal neurons toward holding their current state rather than drifting to other representations. This is the “hold” signal in the hold/update balance that governs working memory gating.

Insufficient dopamine, or dysregulated D1-receptor function, produces:

  • Working memory representations that degrade faster than normal
  • Inability for PFC to sustain its top-down feedback drive long enough
  • Reduced signal-to-noise ratio — background neural noise competes with the intended representation
  • Failure to suppress the DMN during deliberate task states

This is precisely why stimulant medications (which elevate synaptic dopamine and norepinephrine) are effective for ADHD. And notably, some individuals informally report modestly clearer mental imagery on stimulants — a predicted downstream consequence of restoring the PFC hold signal, though no controlled studies have confirmed this yet.

Norepinephrine governs the raw signal-to-noise ratio of PFC neurons independently. Low norepinephrine means the intended mental image is surrounded by more background noise — which may explain why the gap between imagery bursts presents as dark static or restless noise rather than simply nothing.

The DMN Takeover — Same Failure, Two Domains

Imagery drift and thought derailment are the same mechanism

In neurotypical brains, deliberate task engagement — including deliberate imagery — suppresses the DMN. The task-positive network (PFC + parietal) and the DMN operate in anti-phase: when one is active, the other is inhibited. This is one of the most replicated findings in cognitive neuroscience.

In ADHD, DMN suppression fails. The DMN intrudes during tasks, producing thought derailment, motivation-independent mind-wandering, and chains of association that drift from the original goal. Apply this to imagery specifically:

Imagery domain ADHD attention domain
Apple drifts to apple-adjacent, then unrelated, then dissolves Task-thought drifts to task-adjacent, then unrelated — “what was I doing?”
PFC hold weakens → DMN takes over imagery generation PFC hold weakens → DMN takes over attentional content
Image follows emotional salience, not intent Attention follows emotional salience, not task demands

The apple becoming apple-adjacent, then something else, then dissolving is not a quirk of imagery. It is ADHD's attentional drift pattern rendered in visual cortex.

Focus as a Modulator — Phasic Arousal and the Imagery Lift

Why imagery can transiently brighten when something matters

People with low imagery, especially those with ADHD, often report that their mind's eye is not fixed at a single setting. Under strong emotional engagement, novelty, or genuine interest, the image briefly sharpens — closer to average vividness, sometimes momentarily beyond it — and then collapses back to baseline once the catalyst fades. This is consistent with the rest of the page's model if we treat focus as a gain term on the existing PFC → FIN → V1 signal rather than as a separate mechanism.

Three subcortical neuromodulator systems plausibly drive that gain:

  • Locus coeruleus → noradrenaline. Emotionally salient stimuli trigger a phasic NE burst that sharpens PFC signal-to-noise (Aston-Jones & Cohen's adaptive gain theory, 2005). The same system is a primary ADHD treatment target — atomoxetine acts here directly.
  • VTA → dopamine into PFC. Arnsten's inverted-U (2011): PFC working-memory and top-down maintenance peak at a moderate dopamine tone and collapse on either side. Untreated ADHD baseline tends to sit on the low-arousal slope of that curve, so a salient catalyst transiently lifts the system toward its optimum rather than past it.
  • Amygdala → PFC under emotional salience. Emotionally weighted content recruits stronger and more sustained PFC engagement than neutral content — the “hot vs. cold cognition” distinction.

The closest direct experimental fingerprint comes from pupillometry: pupil-linked arousal scales with imagery vividness on a trial-by-trial basis (Kay, Keogh, Smith & Pearson, 2022, eLife). Pupil dilation is a well-validated proxy for locus-coeruleus activity, so the noradrenergic gain story is not just a hypothesis grafted on from the attention literature — it has already been observed inside imagery itself.

Aston-Jones & Cohen, 2005 (Annu Rev Neurosci, adaptive gain) · Arnsten, 2011 (Nat Rev Neurosci, PFC inverted-U) · Kay, Keogh, Smith & Pearson, 2022 (eLife, pupil-imagery coupling) · Wicken, Keogh & Pearson, 2021 (imagery–emotion bidirectionality)

The lived ADHD pattern — low baseline, catalyst-driven lift, fast decay

Mapped onto the modulator picture, the ADHD experience of imagery looks coherent rather than paradoxical:

  • Tonic baseline. PFC tone runs low → the top-down imagery signal is thin → low imagery is the resting state.
  • Phasic catalyst. An emotionally weighted, novel, or intrinsically rewarding target spikes LC-NE and VTA-DA → PFC gain rises → the descending signal lifts → vividness climbs toward average. This is the same machinery that subjectively presents as ADHD hyperfocus.
  • Decay. The catalyst fades; the modulator systems are not built to sustain phasic gain; vividness collapses back toward baseline. The image was not memorised at the brighter setting — it was rendered at it.

The bidirectional finding from Wicken, Keogh & Pearson (2021) tightens the loop: emotion needs imagery to be amplified into a strong physiological response (the skin-conductance result), and emotion is in turn one of the few levers that reliably amplifies imagery in the moment. Imagery and affect drive each other, with PFC gain as the shared throttle.

Two notes of caution to keep this honestly hypothetical:

  • Some of the apparent “lift” under emotional focus may be measurement rather than mechanism — under emotional load the image is attended to more carefully, raising self-rated vividness even if the underlying signal changed less. Pupillometry helps separate these; introspection alone cannot.
  • ADHD is heterogeneous. Inattentive and hyperactive presentations likely sit at different points on the inverted-U, and the size of any phasic lift should differ accordingly.

Testable predictions specific to the modulator account

  • Imagining emotionally salient content (a person who matters, a feared scene) should produce higher trial-level vividness in low-imagery and ADHD participants than imagining neutral content (a generic apple) — a within-subject effect, not just between-subject.
  • That within-subject lift should be tracked by pupil dilation on the same trials, replicating Kay et al. 2022 specifically inside an ADHD sample.
  • Stimulant medication (which raises tonic NE/DA in PFC) should produce a smaller, broader, longer-lasting lift than emotional catalysts do — baseline-shifting rather than spike-shaped.
  • The phasic lift should be larger in inattentive ADHD than in hyperactive ADHD, because the inattentive baseline sits further down the inverted-U.

None of these have been directly tested in an ADHD-plus-imagery sample. All four are falsifiable with existing instruments (VVIQ, eye-tracking, standard ADHD subtype assessment).

Why Hyperactivity Mellows But Imagery Likely Does Not

Two different circuits mature on different timescales

ADHD involves at least two partially distinct neural systems that develop differently across the lifespan. Understanding which one governs which symptom explains why the profile changes with age.

Component Primary circuit Developmental trajectory
Hyperactivity / impulsivity Dopamine in striatum and basal ganglia (motor inhibition circuits) Genuine neurological improvement through the 20s as PFC myelination increases inhibitory control over motor output. This is not just learned management — it is partial circuit resolution.
Inattention / working memory Dopamine in PFC itself (D1 receptors, signal persistence) More persistent into adulthood. The underlying PFC signal-sustain problem does not resolve with the same maturational process. Adults with ADHD typically retain working memory deficits even after hyperactivity fades significantly.
Mental imagery vividness PFC top-down feedback to V1/V2 (working memory subsystem) Lives entirely in the PFC-driven working memory domain — not the striatal/motor domain. Improvement is not expected from the same maturational process that calms hyperactivity.

The circuit that mellows — striatal motor inhibition — is a different circuit from the one driving both inattention and low imagery. Hyperactivity resolving while imagery remains low is exactly what this model predicts.

The Falsifiable Prediction

What this hypothesis specifically predicts

1. ADHD inattentive subtype should show significantly lower VVIQ scores than ADHD hyperactive/impulsive subtype, and both should score lower than neurotypical controls matched for age and IQ.

2. Stimulant medication (methylphenidate, amphetamines) should produce a measurable — if modest — improvement in VVIQ scores in ADHD populations, because it directly restores the dopamine D1 hold signal in PFC.

3. Aphantasia and complete ADHD inattentive presentations should co-occur at meaningfully above-chance rates in a large, controlled sample.

4. The degree of imagery vividness impairment should correlate with working memory capacity scores across both ADHD and non-ADHD populations — indicating a shared underlying dimension rather than two independent traits.

5. Within an ADHD sample, imagining emotionally salient content should produce a measurable trial-level vividness lift over neutral content, and that lift should be tracked by pupil dilation — a within-subject signature of phasic LC-NE / VTA-DA gain restoring the PFC top-down signal toward functional levels.

None of these predictions have been rigorously and directly tested. All are falsifiable. The framework is grounded in established biology and is worth empirical investigation.

Open Questions
Whether the link is a causal relationship (shared PFC mechanism) or merely correlation driven by a third factor such as general attentional capacity.
Whether stimulant medications produce reliably measurable VVIQ improvements — and if so, what the effect size is in practice.
Whether low imagery in ADHD is worse for the inattentive subtype vs. hyperactive/combined subtype, as the model specifically predicts.
Whether non-visual ADHD deficits (verbal, auditory) produce analogous reduced imagery vividness in their respective sensory modalities.
Whether having both low imagery and ADHD produces a distinct cognitive profile compared to either condition alone.
Whether aphantasia individuals without ADHD show PFC connectivity differences — which would help isolate the imagery mechanism from the attentional one.
